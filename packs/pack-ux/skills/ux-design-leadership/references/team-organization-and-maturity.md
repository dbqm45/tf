## 1. Design team structures
| Model | Advantages | Risks | When |
|---|---|---|---|
| Centralized (one team, assignments to projects) | consistency, growth, flexible assignments | design is often an executor, it comes in after the problem is defined | a small number of designers, low maturity |
| Embedded (designers in product teams) | closeness to the product, fast decisions | silos, drifting standards, professional loneliness | high product maturity, a strong shared culture |
| Hybrid (in teams, the management line in design) | influence on the product and consistency | unclear responsibility between design and product | many teams, a need for standards |
The description of the models according to Futurice and authors from projectmanagement.com (accessed 2026-10-17); decide by context, not by fashion.
Whether one product team needs a full-time designer or 0.5 FTE is enough — decide from the data on
workload, not by eye. The order of changes: first the rituals and standards, only then a reorganization.

## 2. DesignOps and ResearchOps
DesignOps is work on how the design team works (people, processes, tools, standards), so that designers can
design. The scope to consider: onboarding, tools and licences, managing assignments and workload, budget,
communication and rituals, quality standards. The canonical frameworks (DesignOps Assembly, NN/g) [unverified — not
opened]. ResearchOps (participant panel, consents, repository) — ux-research-methods. Rituals: a weekly design
critique (the goal, the author's question, comments on the goal and not on taste), a quality review before release, a demo every iteration,
a design retro once a quarter.

## 3. Career and hiring
Levels (e.g. junior, designer, senior, lead, principal) with behaviour criteria in three dimensions: craft,
impact, leadership. Two growth tracks: expert and managerial. Hiring: a task based on a real problem
with a time limit, one rubric, a panel with someone from product. Compare with fe-engineering-levels, so that the levels do not drift
apart from engineering.

## 4. Budget and ROI
The argument in three parts: (1) the cost of absence in your own numbers (support contacts × the cost of a contact,
churn, fixes after release), (2) the cost of the team and tools, (3) the expected effect as a range and
assumptions. Treat external design ROI indicators carefully: give the source and the date or mark them
[unverified]. Measure after release and report to the board every quarter.
