# Parties and relationships — who takes part, in which roles, linked how

## When it applies
- Signal: the same person or organisation appears in several roles (buyer and seller, patient and employee), or
  meaningful links connect it to others (subsidiary, agent, guardian, member of a team).
- Not this: a login account with no business role — an identity module is enough.

## Core shape
- **Party**: a person or an organisation (company, department, branch, team). One technical id for every party type,
  generated by the application — never derived from a natural key (tax ids do not apply to every type, phone numbers get
  reassigned, emails may be unverified).
- **Registered identifiers**: a 0..n set of *type + value* (VAT id, company register number, passport), each type with
  its uniqueness, format and applicability rules (e.g. a company register number only for organisations).
- **Role**: a value held by a party ("customer", "supplier", "lead", "carrier"). Adding the same role twice is a no-op.
  A policy says which roles each party type may hold.
- **Relationship**: its own entity with identity — *from party + role*, *to party + role*, a type, usually a direction,
  optionally a validity window. Managed separately from the parties it connects.
- **Relationship types** (configuration, the "knowledge level"): for each type, which party kinds may stand at each end,
  and optional structure — *must form a tree* (a legal ownership chain), *fixed tiers* (region → area → branch). Party
  kinds may form a hierarchy; a rule on a broad kind (organisation) applies to every narrower kind (branch). This
  catalogue is data that changes behaviour, so it gets its own tests.
- **Scope of a relationship** (optional): what exactly one side has taken on for the other — which products, which
  territory, up to what volume ("services pumps in the northern region, up to 40 sites"). Scope items are typed per
  relationship type, not one universal attribute list.
- **Position** (optional): a party for a job that outlives its holders ("plant safety officer"). Standing
  responsibilities attach to the position; a person *holds* it through a relationship with a validity window. Use it
  only when such responsibilities are substantial and holders change; otherwise link people directly.
- **Contact points**: postal, email, web, phone — each with purposes (billing, delivery, contact) and a lifecycle.
  Usually a separate aggregate keyed by party id.

## Rules
- Organisational hierarchies are relationships (parent ↔ subsidiary, unit ↔ sub-unit), not a `parent_id` column — several
  structures exist at once (legal, cost centres, sales regions).
- Relationship constraints: allowed party types at each end; counts (at most N of a kind); who may relate to whom;
  time rules (minimum duration, seasonal). For each, decide: must hold immediately, or may hold eventually.
- Rules over a whole contact set (exactly one active billing address, at most N changes a month) live with that set;
  keep contact points inside the party aggregate only if a rule needs immediate consistency with party data.
- Permissions that follow from a relationship ("agent A may order on behalf of B", "X leads team T") are queried from
  the relationship graph by the authorisation layer. Global access lists are pure authorisation. Credentials live outside
  the model, linked by party id.
- Operations return success, no-op (duplicate, nothing changed) or failure; events only for real changes.
- Relationships inside the party aggregate are fine for small cases; with counting rules and large graphs they become a
  bottleneck — keep them separate, optionally projected into a graph store for path queries (relational writes + outbox).
- A segment or group is a party only if it has its own identity and accountability (it is invoiced, signs contracts);
  otherwise it is a query or projection.

## Decisions to settle
- Merging duplicates (two records of the same company): who decides, what happens to relationships and history?
  Strategies and identifiers from many issuers → identity and references.
- History and as-of-date queries ("who was the parent company on 1 June?").
- Anonymisation that keeps relationships intact.
- Consent per contact point.
- Does blocking a party cascade to its relationships?

## Belongs elsewhere
- Login, passwords, sessions → identity / be-auth-security.
- Addresses' meaning and enclosing areas → places and zones (the party side owns *who uses which address for what*).
- Contracts between parties → commitments.
- Registered identifiers from several issuers, aliases, duplicate merging → identity and references.

## Traps
- A `User` class with `isCustomer`, `isSupplier` flags and nullable company fields.
- One class or table per role (`Buyer`, `Seller`, `Lead`) for the same real-world company; one application per role.
  All of them model the role instead of the party.
- A tax id as primary key; a phone number as identity.
- `parent_company_id` that cannot express a second, cost-centre hierarchy.
- Relationship types hard-coded as enums with `if` checks on party kinds — a new structure needs a release.
- Responsibilities of a job copied onto each new holder by hand.

## Worked example
Requirement: a B2B marketplace where companies buy and sell; subsidiaries place orders, the parent pays invoices;
some buyers order through a purchasing agent. Today: `Buyer` and `Seller` tables.
- Party `Org-81` (Nordwind Holding), `Org-82` (Nordwind Logistics), `Org-90` (ProcureNow agency) — generated ids; VAT ids
  as registered identifiers.
- Roles: Org-82 → buyer, seller; Org-81 → payer.
- Relationships: `Org-81 (parent) → Org-82 (subsidiary)`, type *ownership*, from 2024-01-01;
  `Org-90 (agent) → Org-82 (principal)`, type *acts on behalf of*, 2026-01-01 – 2026-12-31.
- Contact set of Org-81: exactly one active billing address — the invoice for Org-82's order goes there per the
  *billed-to-parent* relationship rule.
- "May Org-90 place an order for Org-82 today?" — authorisation queries the active *acts on behalf of* relationship.
- Flag: `Buyer`/`Seller` tables are the role-oriented anti-pattern; migration merges rows into parties — duplicate
  merging is an open question (the same VAT id in both tables).
